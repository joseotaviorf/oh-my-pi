§1Executive summary

The fleet already made the big move — x86 Gen 5 → ARM Gen 6 — and the production evidence (AMD_WALL_CORRECTION = 0.735, the 594-DAG median that ARM ran 26.5% faster wall-clock) is decisive: stay on ARM. The open question is which ARM generation. The answer: migrate consolidation_* from Gen 6 → Gen 7 (Graviton3), pilot Gen 8 only for c8g/Java-heavy jobs.

Migrate the consolidation_* t-shirt presets from Gen 6 → Gen 7 (Graviton3) (~9–12% total savings (closed-form, from the EC2/DBU cost-of-work model — live fleet data not provided)); pilot Gen 8 for c8g/Java-heavy DAGs only; stay ARM. Gen 6 → 7 is a robust win: real Spark is ~+15.0% faster while matched-size on-demand price rises only ~6%, so both EC2-hours and DBU-hours shrink. Gen 7 → 8 is marginal for batch Spark (~+8.0% faster vs ~10% pricier).

§3Methodology

Cost-of-work. For a fixed amount of work, cost ∝ ($/hr) ÷ throughput, so perf-per-dollar = throughput ÷ ($/hr). CoreMark-as-throughput is an optimistic upper bound; the Spark-adjusted variant uses the triangulated SPARK_SPEEDUP constants.

Triangulation. CoreMark/$ (CSV) is cross-checked against SPECint2017 per-core (~+12%/gen) and AWS TPC-DS end-to-end Spark runtime (r6g→r7g 1.14×, r7g→r8g 1.085×). Real Spark TPC-DS is the headline; CoreMark is flagged as a 2–3× over-estimate.

Total-cost model (§9). Databricks cost = EC2 (on-demand driver + spot workers) + DBU (priced over wall-clock). A faster node shrinks the wall, so it cuts both EC2-hours and DBU-hours: ec2_new = ec2_old · p / s, dbu_new = dbu_old / s ⇒ total_new/total_old = (e·p + (1−e))/s, with e = fleet EC2 share, p = matched-size price ratio, s = Spark wall speedup. DBU/node-hour is assumed generation-neutral at matched vCPU/RAM (Databricks prices DBU by node size, not silicon gen).

Spot vs on-demand. Workers run on spot; §3 below reports implied spot:OD ratios vs the recommender's flat 0.37.

§5CoreMark vs reality (triangulation)

§6ARM vs x86 — stay ARM

Latest x86 (m8a/c8a/r8a, Gen 8) shows competitive CoreMark/OD$ — m8a even edges m8g on the integer benchmark. But CoreMark is integer-only. The repo's own telemetry measured ARM running 26.5% faster wall-clock on the same DAGs (AMD_WALL_CORRECTION = 0.735, scripts/recommend_cluster_specs.py:92, 594-DAG median), and Databricks Photon-on-Graviton delivers ~3× price-performance with no Graviton surcharge. Generic CoreMark cannot override that production evidence.

§7Gen 8 deep-dive

For batch Spark on m8g/r8g, Gen 7 → 8 is roughly break-even: the ~+8.0% Spark speedup is offset by the ~10% on-demand price bump (see the cost/work G7→8 column in §4 — close to zero, occasionally positive). The total-cost saving comes only from the faster wall trimming DBU-hours: ~1–5%.

Where Gen 8 wins: c8g compute (best CoreMark/$ of the Gen 8 ARM line), per-core/licensing-bound jobs, Java-17 workloads, and DBU-heavy DAGs (where the wall-shrink dominates). Caveat: Databricks supports m8g/m8gd/r8g, but c8g (all compute) is still Photon-blocked by the compute core/RAM-ratio constraint (see cluster_spec_recommender_algorithm.md, "Photon × Instance-Family Constraint") — usable STANDARD only.

§8Mixed-generation clusters (Gen 6 driver + Gen 7 workers)

Databricks lets the driver and worker node types be selected independently and permits any same-architecture mix; only ARM↔x86 mixing is blocked ("Databricks does not support mixing AWS Graviton and non-AWS Graviton instance types", docs.databricks.com/aws/en/compute/configure). Graviton2 (Gen 6) and Graviton3 (Gen 7) are both arm64 under one Databricks Runtime, so an m6g-driver + m7g-workers cluster is allowed.

The wall is worker-set, so worker cost and DBU are identical between all-Gen 7 and mixed — the only delta is the driver's on-demand $/hr (~6%; m6g.large $0.077 vs m7g.large $0.082 = $0.005/hr) on the driver's slice ⇒ +0.5% of total for this non-driver-heavy DAG (<1%).

A ~14%-slower Gen 6 driver lengthens any driver-bound phase (broadcast build, collect, query planning), re-inflating worker-spot and DBU and erasing the saving.

Verdict: default the driver to the worker generation (Gen 7). Mixed-gen pays only to amortize under-utilized Gen 6 Reserved Instances / Savings Plans (sunk-cost capacity), not on the on-demand rate.

§10The recommender gap

The ~+15.0% Gen 7 win is currently unreachable because the recommender is structurally pinned to Gen 6, and so are the Databricks presets:

  • Hardcoded family→Gen 6 nodes. _node_for_family_tier (scripts/recommend_cluster_specs.py:600-602) returns {"compute":"c6g","general":"m6g","memory":"r6g"}.
  • Capacity-then-cheapest selection. _single_node_candidates (:631-642) and _node_for_demand (:704-727) sort/min by ($/hr, memory, vcpus) — Gen 6 always wins at equal vCPU/RAM.
  • No throughput credit. estimate_projected_total_cost (:1007-1088) scales by core count and observed wall but never credits a higher-throughput node's shorter wall.
  • Presets lag. The Databricks consolidation_* presets in prod_conf.yml are Gen 6, while emr_7_12_consolidation_* are already Gen 7 — Gen 7 is vetted in-repo on the EMR side.

The fix is specified, decision-complete, in recommender_multigeneration_migration_plan.md.

§11Recommendation & action items

  • (a) Seed Gen 7 prices + specs and retarget the recommender + Databricks consolidation_* presets to Gen 7 (per recommender_multigeneration_migration_plan.md).
  • (b) Pilot Gen 8 on c8g compute / per-core-bound / Java-heavy DAGs (STANDARD where Photon is blocked).
  • (c) Keep ARM. The 26.5%-faster-wall production evidence plus Photon-on-Graviton price-perf outweighs x86 CoreMark wins.
